### Mofongo

- From: Puerto Rico
- Origin (Wikidata): Puerto Rico
- Ingredients (Wikidata): olive oil, cooking banana, pork, broth, garlic

Fun facts:

- (history) The earliest known written recipes for mofongo appeared in Puerto Rico's first cookbook, El Cocinero Puerto-Riqueño o Formulario, in 1859.
- (about) While mofongo was named, standardized, and published in 19th-century Puerto Rican cookbooks, similar mashed plantain-and-crackling preparations circulated in rural Dominican kitchens under local names before becoming integrated into broader modern restaurant fare.
- (culture) Mofongo is traditionally served with fried meat and chicken broth soup.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) A 1980 booklet from the U.S. government promoting tourism in Puerto Rico wrote of mofongo as being "jocularly described as a Puerto Rican matzoh ball" and described mofongo as being a "mashed, roasted plantain, combine with bacon, spices and goes well with chicken soup".
- (culture) Mofongo is a traditional Puerto Rican dish combining influences from the cultures of the Greater Antilles island, descending from Spain, West Africa, and Taíno, where Puerto Rico gets most of its culture and roots.
- (culture) The similar funche criollo does not include pork, however, one of the key ingredients in traditional mofongo.
- (origin) Mofongo comes from Puerto Rico.
- (about) Not to be confused with Mofongo.
- (history) Another well-known recipe was written by Elizabeth B.K. Dooley in her Puerto Rican cookbook (1948).
- (origin) These cultural influences also resulted in the creation of mofongo's distantly related but notably different West African dish fufu, but mofongo's unique flavor comes from a combination of West African flavors and the Taíno Indian culinary tradition of mashing root vegetables, mixed with Spanish culinary influences.
- (culture) African mortar traditions: Enslaved West and Central African populations brought the practice of mashing starchy foods and plantains in wooden mortars (pilones), related to traditions such as fufu.
- (culture) Pork crackling mash in the central Cibao: In areas with strong pork-processing traditions such as Moca (Espaillat) and La Vega, cooks commonly mashed fried green plantains and garlic together with the crisp remnants and fat left in the rendering pot.
