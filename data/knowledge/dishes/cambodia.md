### Kampot fish sauce

Kampot fish sauce is a variety of fish sauce from Kampot Province, Cambodia produced by fermenting saltwater fish with Kampot sea salt.

- From: Cambodia
- Origin (Wikidata): Cambodia

Fun facts:

- (culture) The largest producer of Kampot fish sauce is E Che Ngov Heng Food Production of Kampot at Ta Ang village, which produces 10,000 litres of fish sauce per day, fermenting it in traditional wooden containers for up to two years.
- (about) In 2021, Ministry of Commerce of Cambodia had created a working group and started the evaluation of Kampot fish sauce as a potential Geographical indication (GI).
- (origin) Kampot fish sauce comes from Cambodia.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (about) In 2022, Kampot Municipality had 10 fish sauce producers and Kampot-Kep Fish Sauce Production Association was in the process of being registered to allow for the recognition of Kampot fish sauce as a GI in order to promote its export.
- (about) On 31 January, 2023, Ministry of Commerce reported that the GI registration for Kampot fish sauce was in the final stages.

### Mondulkiri wild honey

Mondulkiri wild honey is honey harvested by the Bunong honey hunters from giant honey bee nests in the rainforests of Koh Nhek District, Pichreda District, Orang District, Keo Seima District, and Senmonorom in Mondulkiri province.

- From: Cambodia
- Origin (Wikidata): Cambodia

Fun facts:

- (culture) Prior to 2004 when honey gathering in Mondulkiri reached a commercial scale, it was almost exclusively collected for personal use (either household consumption or traditional medicine).
- (about) In December 2017, 3 honey groups had been established – in Krang Tesh (with 90 members), Srae Y (with 40 members) and Trapaeng Khaerm (with 90 members) –, while a fourth group at Pou Chrey with 26 members was in the process of being registered.
- (origin) Mondulkiri wild honey comes from Cambodia.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (about) On 29 January 2021, Mondulkiri wild honey was granted a domestic Protected Geographical Indication by the Ministry of Commerce of Cambodia.
- (culture) The certification ceremony was originally planned on 6 April 2021 but was cancelled due to the outbreak of COVID-19 in Cambodia and eventually took place on 26 March 2022.

### Num kom

Num kom is a traditional Cambodian steamed rice cake made from glutinous rice flour, filled with a mixture of grated coconut, palm sugar, and toasted sesame seeds distinctively wrapped in banana leaves in a pyramid shape.

- From: Cambodia

Fun facts:

- (culture) Along with num ansom, it is one of the most significant traditional cakes in Cambodia, traditionally prepared during major festivals such as Pchum Ben (Ancestors' Day), Khmer New Year, and weddings.
- (culture) It also symbolizes respect for one’s mother and father, known as mea ba, and a practice with origins in first-century Brahmanical beliefs.
- (culture) For Khmers, num kom, along with num ansom, bear a “special spiritual, and sexual, significance." Historian Sambo Manara explains how num kom are a tangible, edible symbol of Uma’s yoni, or vagina, which Khmers may be coy about discussing.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (culture) This is the counterpart to num ansom, symbolizing Shiva’s linga, or phallus/penis.
- (culture) The Royal Academy of Cambodia notes that the presence of the cake in traditional ceremonies dates back to the ancient period, with some documents linking its use to the reign of King Jayavarman VII.
- (culture) The yoni further symbolizes “mothers, women, and water.” Architectural representations of yoni and linga that permeated Khmer temples died out along with the construction of stone temples in the 15th century, but the practice in rice-based desserts continued.
