### Gibanica

Gibanica is a traditional pastry dish popular all over the Balkans and also Central Europe.

- From: Serbia
- Origin (Wikidata): Italy, Serbia
- Ingredients (Wikidata): egg as food, phyllo

Fun facts:

- (culture) The pastry was mentioned in Vuk Stefanović Karadžić's Serbian Dictionary in 1818 and by a Slovenian priest Jožef Kosič in 1828, where it was described as a special Slovenian cake which is "a must at wedding festivities and is also served to workers after finishing a big project".
- (about) It weighed over 1,000 kg, and was applied for inclusion in the Guinness Book of Records.
- (inspiration) In Slovenia, Croatia and Serbia there are festivals dedicated to gibanica.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (culture) The Slovenian festival of Prekmurska gibanica and ham is held in the Slovenian region of Prekmurje, and the Croatian festival of gibanica is held in Igrišće in Hrvatsko Zagorje.
- (culture) One of them, called the Gibanica Festival or Days of Banitsa, is held each year in Bela Palanka.
- (culture) In Serbia, the dish is eaten as breakfast, dinner, appetizer and snack, and is often consumed at traditional events such as Christmas, Easter and Slava.
- (about) The largest gibanica ever made was in the town of Mionica in 2007.
- (origin) Gibanica comes from Italy, Serbia.

### Karađorđeva šnicla

Karađorđeva šnicla is a breaded cutlet dish named after the Serbian revolutionary Karađorđe.

- From: Serbia
- Origin (Wikidata): Serbia
- Since: 1959-
- Ingredients (Wikidata): table salt, flour, egg as food, kaymak, schnitzel

Fun facts:

- (history) According to Stojanović, he first prepared Karađorđeva šnicla in 1956 or 1957, after receiving an order for Chicken Kiev while working at the Golf Restaurant in the Belgrade suburb of Košutnjak.
- (inspiration) Created by Josip Broz Tito's chef Mića Stojanović in 1956 or 1957 as an improvisation of chicken Kiev, it has become a regular staple in Serbian cuisine.
- (about) Kalenjuk Pivarski said that "no [Serbian] dish created some 50, 60 years ago has such popularity" (nijedno jelo nastalo pre nekih 50, 60 godina nema takvu popularnost) as Karađorđeva šnicla.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (inspiration) The breaded cutlet is named after Serbian revolutionary Karađorđe, who was born in the region of Šumadija, where kaymak was mass-produced at the time.
- (history) Stojanović later presented his work, which he had renamed Karađorđeva šnicla, at a Belgrade food competition in 1967.
- (history) Karađorđeva šnicla dates from 1959-.
- (inspiration) Karađorđeva šnicla was created by Mića Stojanović, a retired cooking professor, former head chef at InterContinental Belgrade, and personal chef for Yugoslav president Josip Broz Tito.
- (origin) Karađorđeva šnicla comes from Serbia.
- (history) That year, Stojanović was invited to join Tito's kitchen staff, and later became the statesman's personal chef—a position he held until Tito's death in 1980.
- (history) In the 1990s, he established a restaurant in Zemun where his family has since prepared the breaded cutlet using the classic method.
- (about) Stojanović unsuccessfully tried to patent his original recipe, which has since been adapted to several variations.

### Pasulj

- From: Serbia
- Origin (Wikidata): Serbia
- Ingredients (Wikidata): meat, onion, bell pepper, Apium graveolens var. dulce, Dutch brown bean

Fun facts:

- (history) Pasulj is believed to have roots in the Ottoman Empire, with the Turks believed to have brought beans to the Balkans.
- (about) Another version of the dish using baked beans is known as prebranac.
- (origin) Pasulj comes from Serbia.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (about) In Bulgaria it is known as bob or bob chorba, which literally means "beans" or "bean soup".
- (about) In Hungary, it is known as paszulyleves/bableves (“bean soup”) or paszulyfőzelék/babfőzelék (“bean stew”).
- (about) In North Macedonia, a spicy and thicker variant is known as tavče gravče, and greens such as celery and cabbage are often added.

### Slavski kolač

Slavski kolač is a traditional Serbian ritual bread (a type of yeast leavened bread).

- From: Serbia

Fun facts:

- (culture) Slavski kolač is made for the Orthodox Christian celebration of Slava, a UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
- (culture) It celebrates home liturgy, domesticity, family gatherings, and family tradition.
- (culture) Slavski kolač, along with the candle and a wheat dish such as žito (koljivo), are present at all Slava celebrations and are considered the crucial elements.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (culture) During the coronavirus pandemic, health authorities advised that it was not necessary to take the cake to church, that singing was not necessary for the celebration, and that the gatherings should be limited to household members.
- (culture) Often extended family and friends are invited to the celebration.
- (culture) The custom of Slava and the importance of the celebration cake are found only among Serbs.
- (culture) The cake symbolizes of the body of Jesus, and the wine with which the cake is eaten represents his blood.
- (culture) Traditionally a woman made the bread the day before the celebration, after bathing, dressing in clean clothing, saying the Lord's Prayer, and crossing herself.
- (culture) Tradition calls for the dough to be made with consecrated water.
