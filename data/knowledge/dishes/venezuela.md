### Cachapa

Cachapa is a traditional dish made from maize flour from Venezuela.

- From: Venezuela
- Origin (Wikidata): Venezuela
- Ingredients (Wikidata): maize

Fun facts:

- (origin) Cachapas have a long history, originating in pre-Columbian times when indigenous people would grind corn with a stone and cook it under their fireplaces, a common practice frequently found in Latin America.
- (culture) Cachapas are traditionally eaten with queso de mano (hand[made] cheese), a soft, fresh, white cheese, similar to fresh Italian mozzarella (but not American/Canadian mozzarella).
- (about) Consumers can find cachapas in any type of establishment, from street trucks to high-end restaurants, due to their popularity and significance in the country.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (origin) Many believe that cachapas originated between the American tribes known as chaima cultures.
- (origin) Modern cachapas are made with a combination of corn, milk, salt, water, and sugar, and fillings like roasted pork and queso de mano ("hand[made] cheese") have been introduced by restaurants.
- (origin) Cachapa comes from Venezuela.
- (culture) Others date this traditional dish to the Yanomami, Arawak, and Parias tribe between 500 and 1,800 years ago.
- (about) Like arepas, they are popular at roadside stands.
- (about) In the Llanos Orientales, they are known as arepas de maiz jojoto or tierno (soft corn) In Seville, Spain, there is a similar word in the Chaima dialect, which is registered as an indigenous word "kachapa" to make a sweet arepa for the Chaima (Venezuelan tribe).
- (culture) Today, this product can be bought pre-made or at specific restaurants, but many locals prefer to eat them as street food.
- (about) Not to be confused with Sweet corn arepa.

### Hallaca

Hallaca is a traditional South American dish.

- From: Venezuela
- Origin (Wikidata): Venezuela
- Ingredients (Wikidata): cornmeal, wine, meat, table salt, maize, raisin, chorizo, vinegar, Bixa orellana, almond

Fun facts:

- (history) The name "hallaca" is distinctly Venezuelan, and historical records place it in Venezuela as early as 1538.
- (culture) Hallaca has been described as a national dish of Venezuela.
- (origin) Some speculate it originated from the Orinoquia.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (inspiration) It is said to have been invented by slaves during the colonial era.
- (about) In 2014, despite food shortages affecting the country, the Venezuelan government created a hallaca with a length of around 400 feet, a Guinness World Record.
- (culture) Hallaca is traditionally served during the Christmas season and has several regional variants.
- (culture) The Hallaca is a traditional South American food known by various names and spread throughout the Spanish Empire in the Americas as far south as Argentina in the decades following the conquest.
- (culture) Although in the other countries of the region it is eaten any day of the year with the same name but different preparations, Hallaca is a staple of Colombian and Venezuelan Christmas celebrations.
- (culture) In contrast to Venezuelan tradition, hallacas are popular year-round in Ecuador, and there are several variants across the country's regions.
- (culture) While there are superficial similarities between the hallaca and the tamale, and both dishes reflect the rich history and diversity of their respective cultures, the hallaca is a unique dish with its own history, tradition, and cultural significance in Venezuela.
- (origin) Hallaca comes from Venezuela.
- (history) Its origin is indigenous, but raisins, capers, olives, and sometimes bits of bacon were added in the 16th Century and after by settlers from the Iberian Peninsula.
- (history) The earliest use of the word in the modern sense is in a 1781 document of Italian missionary linguist Filippo Salvatore Gilii.
- (culture) Along with humitas, they are a staple of traditional Ecuadorian cuisine.

### Pan de jamón

Pan de jamón (ham bread) is a typical Venezuelan Christmas bread, filled with ham, raisins and green olives.

- From: Venezuela
- Ingredients (Wikidata): bread, raisin, ham, olive

Fun facts:

- (culture) Pan de jamón appeared in the beginning of the 20th century and slowly became a tradition in the Christmas season in Venezuela and Madeira.
- (history) According to Miro Popić, Venezuelan journalist and commentator specialized in gastronomy, the recipe is believed to be created in December 1905 by Gustavo Ramella, owner of a bakery located in Caracas.
- (history) By 1920 olives, nuts, and capers were added to the filling.

### Tequeño

Tequeño is a fried spear of wheat dough stuffed with semi-hard queso blanco (white cheese), and is a popular meal or snack in Venezuela.

- From: Venezuela
- Origin (Wikidata): Venezuela
- Ingredients (Wikidata): cheese, table salt, flour, dough

Fun facts:

- (inspiration) One of the most widely accepted versions is that they were invented by the Báez sisters, who lived in Los Teques, in 1912.
- (about) On 21 October 2023, in Los Teques, a tequeño of 15.3 m (50.2 ft) was made to commemorate the city's 246th anniversary, beating the previous Guinness World Record of 5 m (16.4 ft).
- (history) Some believe that it actually dates back to the 1700s in the now gone neighborhood of El Teque in Caracas, which is also located in Venezuela.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (inspiration) Another version says that some time in the 19th century an affluent family was vacationing in Los Teques, and one of their cooks served them this dish, which they named tequeños in honor of the town.
- (culture) Tequeños can be eaten for breakfast, as an appetizer, or as a snack at parties and weddings. 21 October is the International Tequeño Day, and it was declared a cultural heritage of Venezuela on 20 April 2023.
- (origin) Due to the influx of Venezuelan immigrants into these countries over the past few years, it has become increasingly popular in Spain and Argentina, among other countries.
- (origin) Tequeño comes from Venezuela.
- (about) They named their improvised creation “quesitos enrollados” (little rolled up cheeses) and started to sell them in the city and, after some time even sold them in Caracas where they gained great popularity.

### Cocuy

Cocuy is a liquor distilled from the fermented juices of the head, body or leaves of agave.

- From: Venezuela
- Origin (Wikidata): Venezuela

Fun facts:

- (about) With a taste similar to other agave-based liquors such as tequila and mezcal, it is known as the Tequila of Venezuela.
- (origin) Cocuy comes from Venezuela.
- (culture) In Aruba, Cucui or Coecoei is a liqueur of indigenous Indian origin traditionally made with agave sisalana (kukwisa) and other ingredients.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (about) The liquor has become popular even in Caracas, with trendy bars serving cocuy-based cocktails.
- (about) Cocuy can be enjoyed neat or with ice and is popular among women.
- (about) Long considered a cheap rural drink (cf. moonshine), since the 2013 collapse of the Venezuelan economy its popularity has boomed compared to more expensive imported competitors.
- (culture) According to tradition, cucui is served at parties, weddings, Christmas, and dande celebrations.

### Harina P.A.N.

Fun facts:

- (history) Harina P.A.N. dates from 1960-.
- (origin) Harina P.A.N. was first made by Luis Caballero Mejías.
- (culture) The traditional preparation of arepa flour is very labor-intensive, requiring soaking, dehusking, and drying maize before pounding it in a large mortar.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) Precooked arepa flour was invented in the 1950s by Dr. Luis Caballero Mejías, a Venezuelan engineer who used the profits from his patent to finance a Technical Schools system.

### Papelón con limón

Papelón con limón (Regional Spanish for: Panela with lemon) is a Venezuelan beverage made with rapadura (raw hardened sugar cane juice), water and lemon or lime juice.

- From: Venezuela
- Origin (Wikidata): Venezuela, Colombia, Costa Rica, Panama
- Ingredients (Wikidata): drinking water, lime, panela

Fun facts:

- (culture) Papelón con limón is usually served during the hottest hours of the day, and commonly offered with traditional Venezuelan food, such as arepas, cachapas or hervidos (rich chicken or beef stew).
- (origin) Papelón con limón comes from Venezuela, Colombia, Costa Rica, Panama.
