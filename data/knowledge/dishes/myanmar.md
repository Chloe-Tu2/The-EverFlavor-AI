### Burmese pork offal skewers

Burmese pork offal skewers, also known as wet tha dok hto are pork offal skewers simmered in light soy sauce, and popularly served as a street food in Burmese cuisine.

- From: Myanmar

Fun facts:

- (history) The earliest documented reference to Burmese pork skewers is a 1941 cartoon illustrated by Ba Gyan.
- (culture) The street food resembles the Indonesian sekba and the braised meats in the Malaysian and Singaporean dish kway chap.
- (culture) Skewers are sold by the stick, and traditionally, street vendors set up stalls around which patrons gather and sit to enjoy.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (culture) This street food first emerged in Yangon Chinatown, and is now ubiquitous in the city.

### Mohinga

Mohinga is the national dish of Myanmar.

- From: Myanmar
- Origin (Wikidata): Myanmar

Fun facts:

- (about) The origins of mohinga are difficult to pinpoint in the absence of extant records.
- (history) The earliest reference to mohinga dates to the Konbaung dynasty, in the poet U Ponnya's alinga verse poem.
- (culture) Mohinga is traditionally eaten for breakfast, but nowadays it is eaten at any time of day.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (about) The person who invented mohinha is Zayar Lin Kyi.
- (history) Burmese history historian Khin Maung Nyunt has concluded that during pre-colonial times, mohinga was likely a commoner's dish, as a formal recipe for mohinga has not been found in royal records or cookbooks.
- (culture) Food processing tools used to ferment rice dating to the Pyu city-states have been discovered, showing that the tradition of making rice vermicelli, the key starch used in mohinga, has a long history.

### Ngapi

Ngapi is a pungent paste made of either fish or shrimp used in Burmese cuisine.

- From: Myanmar

Fun facts:

- (history) The earliest extant record referencing "ngapi" is a Mon stone inscription dating to the 1st century CE.
- (inspiration) Like cheese, it can be distinguished based on main ingredient and regional origin.
- (history) Ngapi makers are also listed in Burmese stone and marble inscriptions dating to 1100s to 1400s.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) The trade of ngapi was economically significant in pre-colonial era, and was submitted as a tributary gift by Mon kingdoms to the Konbaung kingdom.
- (history) The Mon established a trading pattern for ngapi by the 1400s, trading ngapi, salt, and rice in exchange for rubies, benzoin, and musk from the Bamars in Upper Myanmar.
- (history) In 1880, Pho Hlaing, a Konbaung era scholar, noted the purported health effects of ngapi, including enhanced diffusion and digestion of phlegm and bile in the Utubojanasaṅgaha treatise.
