### Injera

Injera, also known as taita, is a sour fermented pancake-like flatbread with a slightly spongy texture, traditionally made of teff flour.

- From: Ethiopia
- Origin (Wikidata): Ethiopia
- Ingredients (Wikidata): water, teff flour

Fun facts:

- (history) Teff consumption became more popular during the Gondar Period (1632-1769) and became a primary staple by early 1750s.
- (about) Although teff and griddle-like trays appeared in Aksumite archaeology, no scholars stated that injera was the staple food in the kingdom of Aksum or that its people consumed teff injera.
- (origin) Injera comes from Ethiopia.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) By the beginning of the 17th century, teff consumption became widespread in the Ethiopian Kingdom.
- (history) The travel narrative of Francisco Alvares, who lived in Ethiopia during the 1520s, indicates that teff was not yet the main grain used in the Ethiopian kingdom at that time.
- (history) Teff consumption spread over a period of 250 years from the 1520s.The reason for teff becoming the staple food during this period was food shortage caused by climate change and wars.
- (about) Early mentions of injera in Ethiopia appeared only in records produced after the fourteenth century.
- (about) A book titled Ser'ata Gebr written by royal chronicles during the time of Empero Zera Yaqob includes lists of items provided during in the court and the term injera appears among the foods recorded.
- (history) Although the word injera was used in the 14th century, it did not refer to present day injera.
- (history) The method of making spongy present-day injera did not start until the 1770s.
- (about) Barradas added teff as one of the cereals cultivated in the Ethiopian Kingdom.

### Kitfo

Kitfo is an Ethiopian traditional dish that originated among the Gurage people.

- From: Ethiopia
- Origin (Wikidata): Ethiopia, Eritrea
- Ingredients (Wikidata): raw meat

Fun facts:

- (about) Kitfo cooked lightly rare is known as kitfo leb leb.
- (culture) In many parts of Ethiopia, kitfo is served with injera, a spongy, absorbent sourdough crêpe-like flatbread made from fermented teff flour; traditional Gurage cuisine replaces this with kocho, a thick flatbread made of the ensete plant.
- (origin) Kitfo comes from Ethiopia, Eritrea.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (about) Kitfo is often served alongside — or sometimes mixed with — a mild cheese called ayibe or cooked greens known as gomen.
- (culture) Kitfo is served on special occasions such as holidays like Meskel on 27 September, the feast celebrating the Finding of the True Cross.

### Wat (food)

Wat or wet or ito or tsebhi is an Ethiopian and Eritrean stew that may be prepared with chicken, beef, lamb, a variety of vegetables, spice mixtures such as berbere (hot variety), and niter kibbeh, a seasoned clarified butter.

- From: Ethiopia
- Origin (Wikidata): Ethiopia
- Ingredients (Wikidata): meat

Fun facts:

- (history) The Spanish Jesuit missionary Pedro Paez who arrived in Ethiopia in 1603 recorded that Emperor Susenyos told him teff was initially eaten by the poor during times of scarcity.
- (about) Although teff and griddle-like trays appeared in Aksumite archaeology, no scholars stated that injera was the staple food in the kingdom of Aksum and that the people consumed teff injera.
- (origin) Wat comes from Ethiopia.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) The travel narrative of Francisco Alvares, who lived in Ethiopia during the 1520s, indicates that teff was not yet the main grain used in the kingdom at that time.
- (history) Jesuit writer and missionary Father Joao de Barradas, who worked in Ethiopia between 1624 and 1633, provided detailed descriptions of royal banquets and courtly meals in Gondar.
- (history) By 1751, the travel account of Prutky describes teff as the prized grain that was most extensively grown and consumed in the Kingdom.
- (history) By the early 1770s, when James Bruce traveled through northern Ethiopia, he noted that teff-based injera had spread widely throughout the region and had become a staple food in the Ethiopian Kingdom.
- (history) The custom of consuming teff spread over a period of 250 years from the 1520s.
- (history) By around 1750 it had become a primary staple in northern Ethiopia.
- (about) Early mentions of injera appeared only in records produced after the fourteenth century.
- (about) A book titled Ser'ata Gebr written by royal chronicles during the time of Empero Zera Yaqob includes lists of items provided during in the court and the term injera appears among the foods recorded.
- (about) Not to be confused with wat.

### Niter kibbeh

Niter kibbeh, or niter qibe, also called tesmi (in Tigrinya), is a seasoned, clarified butter used in Ethiopian and Eritrean cuisine and of Indian origin.

- From: Ethiopia
- Origin (Wikidata): Eritrea
- Ingredients (Wikidata): seasoning, clarified butter

Fun facts:

- (about) Its preparation is similar to that of ghee, but niter kibbeh is simmered with spices such as besobela (known as Ethiopian sacred basil), koseret, fenugreek, cumin, coriander, turmeric, Ethiopian cardamom (korarima), cinnamon, or nutmeg before straining, imparting a distinct, spicy aroma.
- (origin) Niter kibbeh comes from Eritrea.

### Tej

Tej is a honey wine, like mead, that is brewed and consumed in Ethiopia and Eritrea.

- From: Ethiopia
- Origin (Wikidata): Ethiopia, Eritrea
- Ingredients (Wikidata): water, honey, Rhamnus prinoides

Fun facts:

- (history) By the 16th century it was being used to honor warriors, as when Emperor Sarsa Dengel let the hero Aqba Mikael drink tej in his presence.
- (culture) It is generally consumed during social events such as festivals or weddings, and religious events like Ethiopian New Year (Enkutatash).
- (about) Tej is not known to researchers exactly when honey, water and gesho were first mixed together to create tej.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) Prior to the 1900s, tej was only consumed by the King and others in his presence.
- (culture) The social events consist of occasions like inaugural ceremonies, weddings and festivals.
- (origin) Tej comes from Ethiopia, Eritrea.
