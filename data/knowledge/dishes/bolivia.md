### Chola sandwich

The chola sandwich is a Bolivian cuisine sandwich originating in La Paz.

- From: Bolivia
- Origin (Wikidata): Bolivia

Fun facts:

- (history) The chola sandwich developed in the early 20th century from the 1880s Peruvian cuisine sandwich, the butifarra, made from the eponymous botifarra sausage.
- (origin) Chola sandwich was introduced in 1908 by chola street food vendors (caseritas) sold at the southern La Paz park "Parque La Florida", colloquially and eponymically known as "Las Cholas" for the preponderance of chola caseritas; it was an adaptation of their own lunch preparations.
- (about) Per Bolivian ethnologist Antonio Paredes Candia, the botiferra was first interpolated in Bolivia as a beef sandwich topped with chorrillana, a relish of tomato, onion and locoto chilies.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) The chola sandwich began to take form in the 1950s with the addition of llajua and torresmo, then became its modern permutation in the 1970s with the change to pernil over beef.
- (culture) In 2024, a celebration of the gastronomy month included the creation of a two-meter diameter chola sandwich.
- (origin) Chola sandwich comes from Bolivia.
- (inspiration) Chola sandwich is based on butifarra.

### Salteña

A salteña is a Bolivian type of baked empanada, a type of turnover.

- From: Bolivia
- Origin (Wikidata): Bolivia
- Ingredients (Wikidata): meat, dough, gelatin, beef

Fun facts:

- (about) The version that she invented the salteña, as featured in his work, was his own invention.
- (history) In Potosí, a modified version of this dish emerged around the 16th century possibly as a result of the cold weather.
- (culture) In La Paz and Santa Cruz de la Sierra, it is a tradition to enjoy salteñas as a mid-morning snack especially on Sundays, although vendors often start selling salteñas very early in the morning.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) The version that Leonora de Flores created the salteña appears in the novel Potosí 1600 (2002) by Ramón Rocha Monroy.
- (culture) Salteña became a traditional recipe in Potosí and later throughout Bolivia.
- (about) The name 'salteña' refers to the place of origin of one of its famous cooks, Juana Manuela Gorriti, born in Salta province, Argentina.
- (about) Out of desperation, she began preparing "soupy empanadas." The sale of these products became very popular around the same time as Manuela, who was nicknamed "la salteña," referring to her place of origin.
- (origin) Salteña comes from Bolivia.
- (inspiration) In an interview with the author, he said that he was inspired by the story of Leonora de Flores, whose children were dying from the cold in Potosí.
- (history) For this reason, we only refer to Doña Josepha de Escurrechea, whose 1776 recipe book was found and authenticated by researcher Beatriz Rossells Montalvo.
- (history) Historian Antonio Paredes Candia has investigated the origin of this: At the beginning of the 19th century, Mrs. Gorriti, who would later become the wife of President Manuel Isidoro Belzu, had to flee into exile with her family during the government of Juan Manuel de Rosas, settling in the city of Potosí, in Bolivia.

### Llajua

Llajua or llajwa is a Bolivian chili sauce prepared from locotos (Capsicum pubescens) hot chili peppers and tomatoes; sometimes onions are added to the mix.

- From: Bolivia

Fun facts:

- (culture) A traditional use is as a dip for plain cooked potatoes or bread, or an addition to soup prior to the main course.

### Singani

Singani is a Bolivian eau-de-vie or brandy distilled from white Muscat of Alexandria grapes.

- From: Bolivia
- Origin (Wikidata): Bolivia

Fun facts:

- (history) It is generally believed that singani as a name for the distilled spirit arose in that timeframe during the latter half of the 16th Century.
- (about) Singani was first distilled by monastic orders who needing sacramental wine found it expedient to also distill.
- (origin) Grapevines were introduced into the mountain valleys of Bolivia by Spanish missionaries arriving as early as 1530, and the production of wine in Bolivia is first known from these places.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) Singani has been produced since the 16th century shortly after the Spanish arrived in South America.
- (about) Most sources say the name singani derives from a pre-Columbian village of that name near the mission that first distilled the liquor.
- (history) Since the 1990s, formal Bolivian regulations have codified what has long been practiced, and the vineyards from which singani is made are to be planted at elevations of 1,600 m (5,250 feet) or higher.
- (about) Singani is currently not clear which site first became known for producing singani liquor, although there are at least three probable candidates.
- (history) The date range for the initiation of wine production in Bolivia is from about the 1530s to the 1550s (grapevines take a number of years to mature) which corresponds approximately to the initiation of winemaking in neighboring Peru and Chile.
- (about) Singani is thereby known as an altitude product in Bolivian legal terms, the official phrase "of altitude" being also applied to Bolivian wines and grapevine cultivars.
- (origin) Singani comes from Bolivia.
- (inspiration) Based on genetic studies, two seminal varieties of grape were introduced to the Americas as early as 1520 by Spanish immigrants arriving via the Canary Islands where these varieties were well established, Muscat of Alexandria and Mission.
- (history) Spanish explorers under Francisco Pizzaro reached the Inca Empire in 1528.
- (history) By 1538 while Lower Peru was still unsettled due to war, official Spanish cities such as the future archdiocese at Sucre were initiated in Upper Peru or what is today Bolivia.
- (history) In 1545 a massive silver strike was discovered nearby at the Cerro Rico, Potosi.
- (origin) Because of the importance of this site—nearly all of the silver on the Spanish Main originated from there—special attention from the Spanish Crown and allied missions resulted in the establishment of a greater number of religious settlements in the general area.
- (history) Most distilled liquor in the Spanish colonies was called aguardiente, many present-day liquor names in the Americas being adopted only by the 17th Century or later.
