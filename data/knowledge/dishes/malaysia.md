### Ais kacang

Ais kacang or air batu campur, Jawi: ‏اءير باتو چمڤور "mixed ice", often abbr.

- From: Malaysia
- Origin (Wikidata): Malaysia
- Ingredients (Wikidata): Zea mays Saccharata Group, Phaseolus vulgaris, grass jelly, palm sugar, shaved ice, evaporated milk

Fun facts:

- (history) Some earlier variants of ais kacang are no longer sold, such as the ice ball version of the dessert, which was popular during the 1950s and 1960s.
- (culture) Traditionally, an ice shaving machine is used to churn out the shaved ice used in the dessert, originally hand cranked but now more often motorised.
- (origin) Ais kacang comes from Malaysia.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) The dish underwent many different alterations and variants throughout the history of Singapore, where during the 1920s, one version of it was described as shaved ice topped with rose water, lime or liquorice.
- (history) A version of the dish, known as kachang mera using canned adzuki (in Malay: kacang merah "red bean", hence named such) was also sold during the 1930s.
- (history) By the 1940s, the version of the dish described as having "shaved ice heaped into a little mountain", topped with red/adzuki beans, "a Chinese grape-like fruit", syrup, and condensed or evaporated milk, similar to how it is being prepared today, began to appear.
- (history) Early versions of ice kacang was first known to have been prepared around the time before and after the First World War, where costs of manufacturing ice (in Malay: air batu or ais) became more affordable.

### Akok (food)

Akok is one of the famous traditional foods in the east coast of Peninsular Malaysia, notably the states of Kelantan and Terengganu.

- From: Malaysia
- Origin (Wikidata): Malaysia
- Ingredients (Wikidata): egg as food, coconut milk, palm sugar, wheat flour

Fun facts:

- (about) However, there is a savoury variant known as akok berlauk, which include a filling made of minced meat, curry powder, chili peppers and fried onions.
- (culture) Akok is traditionally baked in a brass mould heated with smouldering coconut charcoal, in a similar manner as the bahulu.
- (origin) Akok comes from Malaysia.

### Bakkwa

Bakkwa is a Chinese salty-sweet dried meat product similar to jerky.

- From: Malaysia
- Origin (Wikidata): People's Republic of China
- Ingredients (Wikidata): pork, dried meat

Fun facts:

- (origin) Bakkwa is made with a meat preservation and preparation technique originating from China.
- (culture) Bakkwa is traditionally made of pork, beef, or mutton, which is prepared with spices, sugar, salt and soy sauce, then dried on racks at around 50 to 60 °C (122 to 140 °F) to a final water activity between 0.60 and 0.69.
- (culture) Whereas the traditional bakkwa has a water activity below 0.7, shafu bakkwa tends to be closer to a water activity of about 0.79.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (culture) Bakkwa is very popular in Singapore and Malaysia where it is traditionally eaten during the Chinese New Year.
- (origin) Bakkwa comes from People's Republic of China.

### Banmian

Banmian or pan mee (Min Nan Chinese: pán-mī) is a Chinese noodle dish that consists of handmade noodles served in soup.

- From: Malaysia
- Origin (Wikidata): People's Republic of China, Singapore, Malaysia
- Ingredients (Wikidata): flour, egg as food, anchovy

Fun facts:

- (about) Dry chilli pan mee is a variant which was invented in Chow Kit, Kuala Lumpur, and is often seen in the Klang Valley.
- (culture) The current style is a mix between the traditional methods of Hakka and Hokkien.
- (origin) Banmian comes from People's Republic of China, Singapore, Malaysia.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (culture) Traditionally, the dough is hand-kneaded and torn into smaller pieces of dough (about 5 cm or 2 in).
- (culture) Traditional versions of banmian use egg noodles that are simply a blend of egg, flour, water and salt that is kneaded and then formed into noodles.

### Devil's curry

- Named after: devil

Fun facts:

### Linopot

Linopot (; also known as linongot in various Dusunic dialects) is a traditional native rice dish among the indigenous Kadazan-Dusun, Murut and Rungus of Sabah, Malaysia, commonly prepared using hill rice or sticky rice wrapped in either doringin (terminalia catappa), tarap (artocarpus odoratissimus) or banana leaves.

- From: Malaysia

Fun facts:

- (culture) Popular through the celebration of Kaamatan, the dish is also available through most of Sabah's coffeehouses and restaurants serving traditional foods.
- (culture) Linopot is considered a special traditional food among Sabah's Dusunic and Murutic indigenous peoples.
- (culture) In traditional preparation, hill paddy is the mostly used for the making, where it can be mixed with other starchy crops such as yam, to create different types of rice.
