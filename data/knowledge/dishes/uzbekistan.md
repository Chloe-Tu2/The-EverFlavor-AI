### Samarkand non

Samarkand non or "Samarkand bread" is a traditional bread from Uzbekistan.

- From: Uzbekistan
- Origin (Wikidata): Uzbekistan

Fun facts:

- (origin) It originates from the region of Samarkand.
- (inspiration) A legend tells that one day, the Khan from Bukhara, having tasted the "non" of Samarkand, asked that the best baker of the city be taken with him to make bread in Bukhara.
- (culture) Samarkand non is a very popular bread that accompanies the numerous dishes of the traditional Uzbek cuisine.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (culture) Samarkand non is baked in a tandir, a traditional well-shaped oven.
- (about) In 2022, around 15,000 Samarkand breads were baked every day in Uzbekistan.
- (origin) Samarkand non comes from Uzbekistan.

### Tandyr nan

Tandyr nan is a type of Central Asian bread cooked in a vertical clay oven, the tandyr or tandoor.

- From: Uzbekistan
- Origin (Wikidata): Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Afghanistan

Fun facts:

- (culture) Non is an essential wedding food, and on the day of the wedding, a bride and groom take bites of a non each, and finish it the following morning for their first breakfast as husband and wife.
- (origin) Tandyr nan comes from Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Afghanistan.
- (culture) Newborn babies have non placed under their heads to symbolize long life, and toddlers learning to walk have non placed between their legs to signify wishes of a blessed journey through life.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (culture) A similar tradition is also done when a son leaves for military service or to work or study abroad: the son will take bites of two non and they will be dried and hung on the ceiling until he returns.

### Uzbek plov

Uzbek plov, also known as palov or osh, is a traditional Uzbek rice dish made with meat (typically fatty lamb cuts), julienned carrots, onions, garlic, rice, cumin powder and cooked together in oil or fat.

- From: Uzbekistan

Fun facts:

- (culture) Uzbek plov is regarded as the national dish of Uzbekistan and is of significant importance to the country's history, tradition, and culture.
- (origin) Pilaf is believed to have originated in Persia and throughout history, was spread throughout Central Asia along migration and trade routes in the ancient Silk Road, primarily present day Tajikistan.
- (culture) In 2016, UNESCO recognized not only the culinary aspects of the dish, but also its cultural importance, and transmission of cultural knowledge over many generations.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (culture) The dish and its culture was inscribed into the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, following a decision adopted by Intergovernmental Committee for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage.
- (culture) Over many centuries, it became well established into Uzbek culture and can be found prepared traditionally for weddings, holidays and communal gatherings, often in large quantities as a symbol for hospitality.
- (culture) In Uzbekistan, large-scale plov cooking, typically in open-air settings with a very large kazan, is a traditional way to celebrate and show hospitality in connection with, for example, weddings, national holidays and large communal gatherings.
- (culture) This recognition aims to help promote and conserve the traditions of plov to be passed down within families and communities.
