### Chocolate fish

A chocolate fish or choccy fish is a traditional confectionery item in New Zealand.

- From: New Zealand
- Origin (Wikidata): New Zealand

Fun facts:

- (history) Chocolate fish have been made since at least 1903, though early varieties may not have included marshmallow.
- (culture) Variants of the traditional item exist; a common version is made of solid orange-flavoured milk chocolate.
- (about) In 2019, the chocolate fish was added to the Cadbury Favourites box.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) Marshmallow-based chocolate fish were being produced in Dunedin in 1937.
- (history) For a short period in the late 1990s to early 2000s, Tip Top sold a chocolate fish ice-cream.
- (history) In 1970, Ron Barclay, Member of Parliament for New Plymouth, asked a question in Parliament about the price of chocolate fish.
- (history) In 1996 a "Great New Zealand Cadbury Chocolate Fish Day" was created in May to support the Child Safety Foundation of New Zealand and the Cystic Fibrosis Association of New Zealand.
- (about) Cadbury stopped producing 20g chocolate fish in 2023, stating that they had become less popular with consumers.
- (origin) Chocolate fish comes from New Zealand.

### Goody Goody Gum Drops

Goody Goody Gum Drops is a New Zealand flavour of ice cream made by Tip Top.

- From: New Zealand
- Origin (Wikidata): New Zealand
- Ingredients (Wikidata): Gumdrop, bubblegum ice cream

Fun facts:

- (history) Goody Goody Gum Drops was invented in 1983 by the general director of Tip Top, Murray Taylor.
- (about) At first they all came out as a big lump, and Danish consultants created a mathematical model of the normal distribution of the gumdrops, saying that the goal could not be achieved.
- (origin) Goody Goody Gum Drops comes from New Zealand.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (about) In November 2008, it was reported that, for the year, consumers had consumed 50 million gumdrops in the ice cream.
- (about) In 2016, Tip Top collaborated with Griffin's to create a Goody Goody Gum Drop flavour of the Griffin's Squiggles biscuit.
- (about) In December 2017, Goody Goody Gum Drops "started to disappear" from stores and later returned.
- (about) In 2019, Primo, a flavoured milk producer, created a Goody Goody Gum Drops flavoured milk.
- (about) In October 2023, the Goody Goody Gum Drop flavour returned, joining the 1.2 L (2.5 US pt) Tip Top Crave product range.
- (about) In 2008, Tip Top created a variant of the ice cream flavour on a stick as a limited edition.
- (about) The stick ice cream came back to stores in 2019, this time larger in size.

### Hagelslag

Hagelslag are small, oblong, sweet-tasting chocolate sprinkles or granules, a few millimetres long, which are sprinkled on slices of buttered bread or rusks.

- From: New Zealand

Fun facts:

- (inspiration) According to historical records from the Amsterdam City Archives, Hagelslag was originally invented by B.E. Dieperink, the director of the liquorice sweet company Venco, in 1919.
- (history) Venco's hagelslag did not contain chocolate until 1951.
- (culture) Hagelslag is traditionally eaten by the Dutch for breakfast or lunch.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) Venz, the Netherlands' largest producer of hagelslag products, has been making chocolate hagelslag since 1936.
- (inspiration) Consequently, De Ruijter opted to name their sprinkles based on their flavors, using the term hagel after the flavor (such as in vruchtenhagel).
- (about) Venz and other companies named their chocolate sprinkles chocoladehagel.
- (origin) De Ruijter introduced competition to Venco with their assortment of flavors, including lemon, raspberry, orange and aniseed.
- (about) Not to be confused with fairy bread.

### Hops

Hops are the green flowers (also called seed cones or strobiles) of the hop plant Humulus lupulus, a member of the Cannabaceae family of flowering plants.

- From: New Zealand
- Ingredients (Wikidata): strobilus

Fun facts:

- (history) In Britain, hopped beer was first imported from Holland around 1400, yet hops were condemned as late as 1519 as a "wicked and pernicious weed".
- (origin) Hops used in England were imported from France, Holland and Germany and were subject to import duty; it was not until 1524 that hops were first grown in the southeast of England (Kent), when they were introduced as an agricultural crop by Dutch farmers.
- (culture) It is believed that traditional herb combinations for beers were abandoned after it was noticed that beers made with hops were less prone to spoilage.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) The first documented use of hops in beer is from the 9th century, though Hildegard of Bingen, 300 years later, is often cited as the earliest documented source.
- (history) The first mention of hops being used in German brewing was in 1079.
- (history) Not until the 13th century did hops begin to start threatening the use of gruit for flavouring.
- (culture) In Germany, using hops was also a religious and political choice in the early 16th century.
- (history) According to Thomas Tusser's 1557 Five Hundred Points of Good Husbandry: In England there were many complaints over the quality of imported hops, the sacks of which were often contaminated by stalks, sand or straw to increase their weight.
- (history) Problems with powdery mildew and downy mildew devastated New York's production by the 1920s, and California produced hops only on a small scale.
- (history) As a result, in 1603, King James I approved an Act of Parliament banning the practice by which "the Subjects of this Realm have been of late years abused &c. to the Value of £20,000 yearly, besides the Danger of their Healths".
- (about) The first documented hop cultivation was in 736, in the Hallertau region of present-day Germany.
- (history) Hop cultivation was begun in the present-day United States in 1629 by English and Dutch farmers.
- (about) Not to be confused with Humulus lupulus.

### Instant coffee

Instant coffee is a beverage derived from processed brewed coffee beans that is quickly prepared by adding hot water or milk to powdered solid coffee extracts.

- From: New Zealand

Fun facts:

- (history) The invention for powdered soluble instant coffee was filed on 28 January 1889 and patented in 1890, by David Strang of Invercargill, New Zealand, under patent number 3518, and sold as "Strang's Soluble Coffee Powder", citing the patented "Dry Hot-Air" process.
- (origin) In the 1950s, General Foods introduced an instant coffee product made from 100% coffee solids without the need for added carbohydrates such as maltodextrins.
- (about) The global instant coffee market was valued at US$4.16 billion in 2026.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) Agglomeration of spray dried coffee has been used since around 1968 to form the instant coffee into granules.
- (history) The product was invented in 1890 in Invercargill, Southland, New Zealand, by David Strang.
- (history) The brand Camp Coffee, a liquid coffee and chicory essence, was first produced in 1876 by Paterson & Sons Ltd in Scotland.
- (history) The invention had previously been attributed to Satori Kato, a Japanese scientist working in Chicago in 1901.
- (history) George Constant Louis Washington developed his own instant coffee process shortly thereafter, and first marketed it commercially (1910).
- (history) The first freeze-dried instant coffee was launched in 1963 by General Foods under the brand Maxwell House.
- (about) Some modern sources have credited French humorist and writer Alphonse Allais with the invention.
- (origin) Kato introduced the powdered substance in Buffalo, New York, at the Pan-American Exposition.

### Pāua

Pāua is the Māori name given to four New Zealand species of large edible sea snails, marine gastropod molluscs which belong to the family Haliotidae (in which there is only one genus, Haliotis).

- From: New Zealand

Fun facts:

- (about) Pāua is known in Australia and the United States as abalone, and in the United Kingdom as ormer shells.
- (history) The industry was started in New Zealand in the 1980s, and to date there are fourteen pāua farms operating throughout New Zealand, from Whangārei to Stewart Island.
- (about) The first farms consisted of small-scale backyard and shed farms in which techniques and processes that grew good-quality pāua were developed; these are now being used on a commercial scale in large pāua farms.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) Pāua shells make up the decoration on Xena's first chakram in the hit TV series Xena: Warrior Princess.
- (about) In New Zealand, these are known as pāua, which (as is the case with nearly all Māori words) is both singular and plural.
- (about) In 2023 the New Zealand pāua export industry was estimated by Fisheries New Zealand to be worth $55 million per annum.
