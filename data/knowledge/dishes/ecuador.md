### Fanesca

Fanesca is a soup traditionally prepared only on Good Friday and is eaten by households and communities in Ecuador.

- From: Ecuador
- Origin (Wikidata): Ecuador
- Ingredients (Wikidata): Cucurbita ficifolia

Fun facts:

- (culture) By the 19th century Quito was already celebrating Holy Week with the dish technically known as Fanesca, which would become an essential component of the event.
- (history) The oldest reference to a Fanesca recipe could be from 1882, described by Juan Pablo Sanz in his book "The Cook's Manual" Currently, Fanesca is most popular in the northern highlands of Ecuador.
- (origin) Fanesca comes from Ecuador.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (culture) This dish is an Ecuadorian tradition that is prepared to give thanks to God for the food provided and blessings.
- (culture) The indigenous people prepared for this festival by fasting and abstaining from sexual activity.
- (culture) The indigenous people who occupied the territory of Ecuador celebrated the Muchuc Nina (New Fire Day) in the season that corresponds to March, where they grilled tender grains with Andean pumpkins taking advantage of the beginning of the young harvest.
- (culture) The Mushuc Nina was celebrated to commemorate the equinox solstice, when the sun takes a perpendicular position on the equinoctial line, erasing all shadows.
- (about) The indigenous people's culinary preparation with tender grains and Andean pumpkins on New Fire Day became known as Uchucuta, a Quichua phrase that means tender grains cooked with chili and herbs, probably accompanied by wild guinea pig meat.
- (culture) During the colony's evangelizing period, the Spaniards mixed Catholic symbols and beliefs with indigenous components in order to accomplish cultural miscegenation.
- (about) Its popularity decreases to the south and the Coastal region.

### Helado de paila

Helado de paila is a creamy frozen dessert with an intense fruit flavor.

- From: Ecuador

Fun facts:

- (origin) Originating in the Andean region of South America, it is made by hand in a large bronze basin, known as a paila, resting on a container of ice and salt, using various aromatic fruits.
- (about) Helado de paila was invented during a period before refrigeration, and it was made using ice that formed at low temperatures in the inter-Andean moors.
- (culture) Today, helado de paila continues to be made at various Colombian and Ecuadorian tourist sites, markets, and festivals, in flavors like blackberry, soursop, naranjilla, coconut, strawberry, tree tomato, mango, and passion fruit.

### Seco (food)

The seco is a stew typical of Peruvian and Ecuadorian cuisine.

- From: Ecuador
- Ingredients (Wikidata): meat, beef, lamb meat, chicken as food

Fun facts:

- (inspiration) This is a myth, since, according to Modesto Chávez Franco in his Chronicles of Guayaquil, there are records of this dish from 1820, almost a century before the English presence in the Santa Elena Peninsula.
- (about) The place where it was created for the first time is not known exactly.
- (history) A recipe for seco de cabrito, a typical dish of the gastronomy of the north coast of Peru, already appears in the New Peruvian Cooking Manual of 1926.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (origin) One of the most widely accepted references is that the name seco comes from the Santa Elena Peninsula, where at the beginning of the 20th century oil works were carried out in the Ecuadorian town of Ancón.
- (history) In the 1930 book Chronicles of Old Guayaquil, Modesto Chávez Franco reports that the dry chicken was already prepared in Ecuador since the beginning of the 19th century.
- (about) Seco is popularly thought that when cooking the stew the water must evaporate until it reaches a degree of dryness, hence it must be "seco", although in reality all the variants of "seco" are usually quite juicy, and that the name "seco" is an ironic way of calling a soupy dish.

### Colada morada

Colada morada is a drink that is part of Ecuador's gastronomic culture along with t'anta wawa bread dolls.

- From: Ecuador
- Origin (Wikidata): Ecuador

Fun facts:

- (culture) Being the Colada Morada as a symbol of a happy journey from life to death.
- (inspiration) As a great example in which they celebrated with the colada morada based on the blood of the llamas is the Quitu-Cara culture.
- (origin) Colada morada comes from Ecuador.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (culture) This drink is traditionally consumed on November 2, a holiday called All Souls' Day or Day of the Dead, along with the so-called t'anta wawa (bread usually of non-ordinary flavor and various fillings that has the shape of a doll, hence the name) which are representations of the dead wrapped in a blanket.
- (culture) Indigenous people from the Ecuadorian mountain range celebrated the rainy season and in turn worshipped their relatives who had died.
- (culture) For the same reason, they exhumed their dead and shared with them this traditional drink.

### Pinol

Pinol or piñol is a traditional hot beverage of Peru, made from máchica (toasted barley flour) and panela (unrefined sugar) mixed with spices and combined with a liquid, usually milk.

- From: Ecuador
- Origin (Wikidata): Ecuador
- Ingredients (Wikidata): milk, spice, panela, máchica

Fun facts:

- (about) The invention of pinol is attributed to Rafael Emilio Madrid of Lima, Peru.
- (inspiration) As the story goes, Madrid was inspired by watching laborers sucking on pieces of panela, and decided to grind panela and mix it with máchica and spices.
- (origin) Pinol was first made by Rafael Emilio Madrid.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (about) As of 2009, the fourth generation of Madrid's family was still involved in producing pinol mix.
- (culture) Despite its relatively recent mid-20th-century origin, pinol is considered a traditional food.
- (origin) Pinol comes from Ecuador.
