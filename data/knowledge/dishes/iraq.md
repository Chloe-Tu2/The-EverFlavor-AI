### Gaz (candy)

Gaz is an Iranian nougat that originated in the Isfahan region.

- From: Iraq
- Origin (Wikidata): Iran, Iraq
- Ingredients (Wikidata): Pistacia vera, Tamarix, rose water

Fun facts:

- (history) Similar confections known as halva have been made in Persia since around the 9th century Saffarid period.
- (about) It is widely known as Persian Nougat in American and European countries.
- (culture) Gaz is made from egg whites, sugar, pistachios and/or almonds, rosewater, and (traditionally) psyllid honeydew collected from Persian manna.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (culture) Gaz has a mild laxative effect, traditionally used for children.
- (culture) Gaz was traditionally (though mistakenly) understood as the sap of the Persian manna Astragalus brachycalyx, sometimes further mistaken by its shared Persian name with the tamarisk Tamarix gallica.
- (culture) The modern form of gaz dates backs 450 years to the Safavid period, the honeydew found on Persian manna being combined with egg white, sugar, glucose syrup, rosewater, and pistachios and/or almonds in traditional and industrial workshops.
- (origin) Gaz comes from Iran, Iraq.
- (about) Not to be confused with Gaz.

### Kahi (food)

Kahi is an Iraqi breakfast dish made from layered flaky filo pastry often served for breakfast with a type of clotted cream called geymar, which is made from water buffalo milk.

- From: Iraq
- Origin (Wikidata): Iraq
- Ingredients (Wikidata): phyllo

Fun facts:

- (history) The name "kahi" is attested in Ottoman sources by the 17th century, referring to a multi-layered pastry sold in markets and served at the Ottoman court.
- (culture) Iraqi Muslims eat kahi on breakfast to celebrate Eid al-Fitr typically after the Eid prayers on the first day, as well as on Fridays, and other official holidays.
- (origin) Kahi comes from Iraq.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) In Baghdad in the 20s, Iraqi historian Abbas Baghdadi describes bakers making kahi with geymar in 1920s Baghdad, as well as making savory varieties filled with meat and vegetables.
- (history) The New Arab reported that during the 1940s, there were only 5 kahi shops in the entirety of Baghdad.
- (history) An early mention of kahi can be found in Iraqi historian Jalal al-Hanafi's 1966 "Dictionary of Baghdadi Industries and crafts".
- (culture) Kahi is eaten on religious holidays, it's traditionally eaten in shops specialising in selling kahi, but also sometimes made at home.
- (culture) Baghdadi Jews and the Iraqi Jewish diaspora celebrate Shavuot by preparing kahi at home eating it with geymar.
- (culture) Beside being prepared in specialized shops, it is also traditionally prepared by women at home.

### Laffa

Laffa, also known as lafa or Iraqi pita, is the Modern Hebrew term for a large, thin flatbread with an Iraqi origin.

- From: Iraq
- Origin (Wikidata): Israel

Fun facts:

- (history) Members of the Jewish community of Iraq, almost all of whom came to Israel via Operation Ezra and Nehemiah in the mid-20th century, brought with them the standard Iraqi flatbread known in Baghdad Jewish Arabic as ʿēsh tannūr, ḫobz al-tannūr, or simply khubz "bread".
- (culture) Laffa is a simple bread that is traditionally vegan and cooked in a tannur or tabun, both of which are clay ovens.
- (about) Laffa is known as Iraqi pita, given its origin in Iraq.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (culture) Laffa is also the traditional bread used in sabich, an Israeli eggplant sandwich.
- (culture) Laffa was traditionally baked in communal outdoor wood or coal-fired ovens and served as an accompaniment to myriad dishes.
- (origin) Laffa comes from Israel.

### Kaymak

Kaymak, also known as qishta or sarshir, is a creamy dairy food similar to clotted cream, made from the milk of water buffalo, cows, sheep, or goats in Central Asia, most Turkic regions on the Eurasian Steppe, the Caucasus, Turkey, most Balkan countries, the Levant, Iran and Iraq.

- From: Iraq
- Origin (Wikidata): Central Asia
- Ingredients (Wikidata): goat milk, sheep milk, water buffalo milk, cow's milk, milk

Fun facts:

- (about) Early Chinese historical sources during the first century BCE record the preparation of a skimmed cream called sū made from cow and sheep milk, noting that it was adopted from Central Asian pastoralists that lived along the northern and northwestern frontiers of modern-day China.
- (history) During the Qing dynasty, Chinese imperial texts reveal the preparation of sū: repeatedly boiling milk, stirring it, allowing it to cool, and lifting the dense skin off the top to make sū.
- (culture) The traditional method of making kaymak is to boil the raw milk slowly, then simmer it for two hours over a very low heat.

More fun facts (when the user asks for more):

- (history) Preserved clotted cream has been recovered from Mongol-period burials dating to around the 13th century.
- (about) In Iran, kaymak is also known as سَرشیر (sarshir), which translates to "top of the milk", and likewise as սեր (ser) in Armenia.
- (origin) Kaymak comes from Central Asia.
- (about) Not to be confused with dulce de leche.
